My favorite definition of a startup comes from Steve Blank. In his words, "startups are the search for a repeatable, scalable business model". I remember reading this and feeling my perspective shift - startups aren't little versions of big companies. Startups are about the search (zero to one), while big companies are about optimization (1-100). 

Sales is likely the most valuable activity a startup will do. Why? Because it's a confrontation with reality and immensely beneficial. Either you make a sale and receive money or you don't make the sale and you've learned something about your customers, pricing, and market need - both are useful! 

Founder has to make decision on over committing - seeing value in new ideas

When the founder does sales they are coming with the highest level of conviction and subject matter expertise. If the founder can't make the sale, there's a good reason. The reason that the founder lost a prospect, is just as valuable as the revenue! Remember,  knowledge is equivalent to money since it's less burn to get to profitability. \
\
- Listening for new market opportunities\
- Changing the price to better fit the need\
- Pitching the product to a new persona\
- Changing the product/service as needed

As the founder hires sales people, this feedback loop gets diluted by incentives. Would you trust a sales person that says the price is too high? Of course not, it sounds like typical sales person excuse - and so change isn't made. When the founder experiences it, they actually have the authority to make the change. The difference is the speed of that feedback loop. Founder-led sales means your company is learning and applying lessons in near real-time. Jessica Livingston of YC [wrote an post](https://blogs.wsj.com/accelerators/2014/06/03/jessica-livingston-why-startups-need-to-focus-on-sales-not-marketing/) about "Why starts need to focus on Sales not Marketing" that succinctly conveys why this happens:

> So why wouldn’t all founders start by engaging with users individually? Because it’s hard and demoralizing. Sales gives you a kind of harsh feedback that “marketing” doesn’t.

Humans, especially optimist-wired founders, are loathe to hear why their startup/idea/life choice doesn't work. Founders need to maintain some of that reality distortion bubble, but also have the grounding to realize that their approach could be wrong. The most classic mistake that technical founders make is that they hire a proven VP of Sales from a large company. "They did it at X, so they can sell here" .
