The best definition of a startup I've seen comes from Steve Blank. In his oft-quoted words, "Startups aren't little versions of big companies. A startup is a search for a repeatable, scalable business model". I like it because it emphasizes repeatable \*before\* scalable. As a founder who's failed a few startups, I have the scars that come from trying to scale something before proving a repeatable business model. A repeatable business model gives you time as it switches your company trajectory from "default dead" to "default alive". \
\
I'm convinced that the path to "default alive" runs straight through the Sales function at the startup. Especially at the early stages of a startup, I'd argue Sales is **the most valuable** activity a startup will do. Why? Because it's the only time your hypothesis confronts reality! As a founder, you'll want to wait until you're "ready" to sell. AKA you don't want to get your own feelings hurt when a prospect says no. But Sales isn't just how you keep the business going afloat, it's the feedback mechanism for your entire approach. \
\
At YC's W19 Bootcamp, I presented my favorite sales maxim to first time founders: 

> "Either you make the sale and get that money or you don't and you learn more about your customers - both are a win!" 

When a founder can't make a sale, there's something wrong and real learning to be had. Founders have highest level of conviction, expertise, and understanding about the prospect. Each lost customer brings the opportunity to learn and adjust everything. But doing that requires some reflection - why didn't this customer purchase? Pricing? Features? Are you creating enough value? Most founders dismiss each lost sale by telling themselves "well they're just not our target demographic". Perhaps that's true... but then it's on you to update your target prospect and see if you're right (by closing a sale). \
\
The **reason** you lose a prospect, is just as valuable as the revenue you would have received! Embedded in that lost reason is the opportunity to close more deals in the future. Founders themselves have to do this work, not hire away the problem with a VP of Sales. Why? Because as a founder, you're the only one with enough power to actually make a change to the product, pricing, or the target customer. Founder-led sales means your company can learn and apply those lessons in real-time across your company. Jessica Livingston of YC [wrote an post](https://blogs.wsj.com/accelerators/2014/06/03/jessica-livingston-why-startups-need-to-focus-on-sales-not-marketing/) about "Why starts need to focus on Sales not Marketing" that succinctly conveys why this happens:

> So why wouldn’t all founders start by engaging with users individually? Because it’s hard and demoralizing. Sales gives you a kind of harsh feedback that “marketing” doesn’t.

Humans, especially optimist-wired founders, are loathe to hear why their startup/idea/life choice doesn't work. However, the best founders maintain their future reality distortion bubble, but are grounded enough in the present to admit when their approach is off and experiment. Even roombas learn when they bump into an obstacle. As a founder you should be slightly smarter than a roomba. To get your startup to "default alive" treat Sales like a learning function and get closer to ending your search. \
